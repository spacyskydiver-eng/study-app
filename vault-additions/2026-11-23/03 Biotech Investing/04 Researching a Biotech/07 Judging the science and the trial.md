# Judging the science and the trial

> [!path] [[Biotech Investing]] › [[Researching a Biotech]]

- This is the step most private investors skip, and where knowledge from the degree counts

## Questions to ask about the drug
1. **Mechanism**: is the target clearly linked to the disease? (Human genetics backing a target, as with PCSK9 or CFTR, raises the odds)
2. **Validation**: has another drug on the same target or pathway already worked? A proven class (e.g. cardiac myosin inhibitors for HCM) lowers science risk but raises competition
3. **Data so far**: how many patients, against placebo or not, and how big was the effect? Small open-label studies often look better than the final result
4. **Endpoint**: is the [[Primary endpoint]] something regulators accept for approval, or a surrogate marker?
5. **Safety**: any serious side effects in the data or the risk factors? A good effect with a safety signal can still fail (murepavadin reached Phase 3 and then failed on kidney damage)

## Odds of reaching approval
Approximate industry averages, from entering each phase to approval:

| Stage | Chance of approval |
| --- | --- |
| Phase 1 | about 8% |
| Phase 2 | about 15% |
| Phase 3 | about 50% |
| Filed with the FDA | about 90% |

- Rare diseases and drugs with strong genetic backing do better; neurology and psychiatry do worse

## Competition and the [[Standard of care]]
- A new drug must beat what doctors already use, on effect, safety, or convenience (a pill instead of an injection, monthly instead of daily)
- Example: Kailera (KLRA) makes an obesity injection in Phase 3, but Lilly and Novo already sell similar drugs. Its drug needs a clear advantage to win share
- Example: SpyGlass (SGP) puts a glaucoma drug on the lens used in cataract surgery, so patients need no daily eye drops. The advantage is convenience and adherence, not a new mechanism

## Sources
- ClinicalTrials.gov: design, size, endpoints and expected end dates
- PubMed: papers on the drug or its class
- Conference abstracts and the company's own data presentations (in 8-K filings on EDGAR)

## Questions
> [!question]- Roughly what share of drugs entering Phase 2 reach approval?
> About 15%.

> [!question]- Why does a proven drug class lower one risk but raise another?
> The science is more likely to work, but the new drug faces approved competitors and must be clearly better.

> [!question]- Why can small open-label trial results be misleading?
> With no placebo group and few patients, chance and expectation effects can make the drug look better than it is.
