# Chromosomes and Genomes (Lectures 12 to 14): questions

> [!path] [[Molecular Biology and Genetics]] › [[Chromosomes and Genomes (Lectures 12 to 14)]]

## 51 questions

### Learning outcomes and genetic material

> [!question]- What is genetics?
> The branch of biology concerned with heredity and variation.

> [!question]- Name the four types of viral genome.
> ssDNA, dsDNA, ssRNA (positive or negative sense) and dsRNA.

> [!question]- What is the difference between positive-sense and negative-sense ssRNA?
> Positive-sense RNA can be translated directly; negative-sense RNA must first be copied, and the complementary (positive-sense) copy is translated.

> [!question]- In the Avery, MacLeod and McCarty experiment, which treatment stopped transformation, and what did that show?
> Treating the IIIS extract with DNase (deoxyribonuclease); so the transforming factor is DNA.

### Genome analysis by shotgun sequencing

> [!question]- What is whole genome (shotgun) sequencing?
> The genome is broken into many random overlapping fragments, each is sequenced, and computer programs assemble them by their overlaps into contigs and whole chromosomes.

> [!question]- What is a contig?
> A continuous stretch of sequence assembled from overlapping sequenced fragments.

> [!question]- What are the three steps of contig building?
> Compare sequences, join sequences, edit.

### Prokaryotic chromosomes and genomes

> [!question]- How big is the E. coli genome, and how many genes does it have?
> 4,639,221 bp (about 4.6 Mb) and about 4300 genes.

> [!question]- Give three features of how genes are arranged in a prokaryotic chromosome.
> Genes are tightly packed, coding sequences are on both strands, and coding sequences can overlap.

> [!question]- In prokaryotes, how are genome size and gene number related?
> They are closely correlated: bigger genomes have proportionally more genes.

> [!question]- How is the 1.2 mm E. coli chromosome fitted into a 1 µm cell?
> By supercoiling: the underwound circular DNA twists on itself into compact supercoils.

> [!question]- What was the first bacterial genome to be sequenced?
> Haemophilus influenzae, in 1995.

### Eukaryotic chromosomes

> [!question]- What is a nucleosome made of?
> A histone octamer (two each of H2A, H2B, H3 and H4) with 147 bp of DNA wrapped around it.

> [!question]- What does histone H1 do?
> It binds the linker DNA between nucleosomes and helps pack them into the 30 nm solenoid fibre.

> [!question]- List the levels of DNA packaging from the double helix to the metaphase chromosome.
> DNA (2 nm) → nucleosomes (11 nm) → solenoid/30 nm fibre → looped chromatin fibre (300 nm) → chromatid (700 nm) → metaphase chromosome (1400 nm).

> [!question]- Name the four chromosome types based on centromere position.
> Metacentric (middle), submetacentric (between middle and end), acrocentric (close to end), telocentric (at the end).

> [!question]- What are the p and q arms of a chromosome?
> The short (p) and long (q) arms on either side of the centromere.

> [!question]- Where is DNA found in a eukaryotic cell?
> In linear chromosomes in the nucleus and in circular chromosomes in mitochondria (and plastids).

### Genome organisation and ploidy

> [!question]- What is the difference between autosomes and sex chromosomes?
> Autosomes are the chromosome pairs that are the same in both sexes; sex chromosomes (X and Y in humans) determine sex.

> [!question]- Define the haploid number (n).
> The number of different chromosomes: the different autosomes plus half the sex chromosomes; half the diploid number.

> [!question]- What is the human haploid number and genome size?
> n = 23; about 3.3 × 10⁹ bp.

> [!question]- Distinguish aneuploidy from euploidy.
> Aneuploidy is 2n plus or minus whole chromosomes (e.g. trisomy 2n + 1); euploidy is whole multiples of the haploid set (2n, 3n, 4n).

> [!question]- Distinguish autopolyploidy from allopolyploidy.
> Autopolyploidy: multiple copies of the same genome; allopolyploidy (amphidiploidy): multiple copies of different genomes, e.g. Brassica napus from B. rapa and B. oleracea.

> [!question]- What is unusual about human chromosome 2?
> It formed by the fusion of two ape chromosomes.

### Genome size and repetitive DNA

> [!question]- What is the C value, and what is the C value paradox?
> The C value is the DNA content of the haploid nuclear genome; the paradox is that it does not correlate with organism complexity or gene number in eukaryotes.

> [!question]- Roughly what percentage of the human genome is functional genes?
> About 2%.

> [!question]- What is satellite DNA, and where is it found?
> Highly repetitive non-coding tandem repeats; the main component of centromeres and other heterochromatin.

> [!question]- Distinguish minisatellites from microsatellites.
> Minisatellites (VNTRs) are 1–5 kb blocks of 15–100 bp repeats; microsatellites (STRs) are 2–5 bp motifs repeated 5–50 times, such as (CA)n.

> [!question]- What are SINEs and LINEs? Give an example of each.
> Interspersed retrotransposons: SINEs are short (< 500 bp), e.g. Alu (~300 bp, >1 million copies); LINEs are long, e.g. L1 (6.4 kb, ~40,000 copies).

> [!question]- Why are rRNA genes present in many copies?
> Cells need large amounts of rRNA, so the 28S/18S/5.8S gene cluster is repeated in tandem hundreds of times.

### Human sex determination

> [!question]- How do Klinefelter and Turner syndromes show that the Y chromosome determines sex?
> Klinefelter (47, XXY) individuals are male despite two X chromosomes; Turner (45, X) individuals are female with no Y; so the presence of a Y determines maleness.

> [!question]- What does SRY stand for, and what does it encode?
> Sex-determining region Y; it encodes testis-determining factor (TDF).

> [!question]- Give two pieces of evidence that SRY is the male-determining gene.
> XY females have a Y chromosome missing SRY; XX mice given an Sry transgene develop as males.

> [!question]- When is SRY expressed and what does it cause?
> From 6 to 8 weeks of embryo development; undifferentiated gonads become testes.

> [!question]- What are the pseudoautosomal regions?
> Regions at the ends of the Y chromosome that are homologous to the X and pair with it in meiosis.

> [!question]- Distinguish euchromatin from heterochromatin.
> Euchromatin is less condensed, contains most genes and is transcribed; heterochromatin is densely packed and its genes are suppressed.

### Dosage compensation and X inactivation

> [!question]- What is dosage compensation, and how is it achieved in humans?
> Equalising X-linked gene expression between XX females and XY males; one X in each female cell is inactivated.

> [!question]- How many Barr bodies are there in cells of a 47, XXY individual and of a 47, XXX individual?
> 47, XXY: one; 47, XXX: two (number of X chromosomes minus one).

> [!question]- What is XIST, and how does it inactivate an X chromosome?
> X inactivation specific transcript, a gene in the X-inactivation centre; its RNA has no ORF and coats the chromosome it is made from, silencing it.

> [!question]- State the Lyon hypothesis.
> X inactivation happens early in development and which X is inactivated in each cell is random, so females are mosaics of cells expressing different X chromosomes.

> [!question]- Why are calico cats almost always female?
> The coat-colour alleles are on the X; a female heterozygote has patches where different Xs are inactivated, giving the mosaic pattern; males have only one X.

### Gene mutations

> [!question]- Distinguish a transition from a transversion.
> A transition swaps a purine for a purine or a pyrimidine for a pyrimidine (A↔G, C↔T); a transversion swaps a purine for a pyrimidine or vice versa.

> [!question]- Distinguish missense and nonsense mutations, with an example of each.
> Missense changes one amino acid, e.g. AAG (Lys) → AGG (Arg); nonsense creates a stop codon, e.g. AAG (Lys) → TAG (stop).

> [!question]- Why do frameshift mutations usually make a truncated protein?
> Shifting the reading frame changes every codon after the mutation and usually soon introduces a stop codon 3′ to the mutation.

> [!question]- What mutation causes the pea le allele and what is its effect?
> An A → G transition changing Ala229 to Thr (A229T) in gibberellin 3β-hydroxylase, leaving very little enzyme activity: a loss-of-function allele.

> [!question]- Explain why Ras G12V is oncogenic.
> It lacks GTPase activity, so it cannot hydrolyse GTP and stays locked in the active form, continuously signalling cell division: a gain-of-function mutation.

> [!question]- Which kind of new allele do mutations most often produce?
> Loss-of-function alleles; gain-of-function alleles are rare.

### Chromosome aberrations and gene duplication

> [!question]- How can unequal crossing over cause a duplication?
> If homologous chromosomes are misaligned when they cross over, one product gets an extra copy of a segment (duplication) and the other loses it (deletion).

> [!question]- How does a chromosomal inversion form?
> A chromosome loops, breaks at two points and the segment between rejoins in the reverse orientation.

> [!question]- Why is gene duplication important in evolution?
> One copy keeps the original function while the other can accumulate mutations and evolve a new function.

> [!question]- Give an example of a gene family that arose by duplication, and how its products differ.
> The globins: myoglobin, α-globins and β-globins (ε, γ, δ, β); adult haemoglobin is 2α2β, fetal haemoglobin is 2α2γ.
