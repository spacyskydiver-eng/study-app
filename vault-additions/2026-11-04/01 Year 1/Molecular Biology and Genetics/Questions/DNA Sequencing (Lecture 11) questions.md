# DNA Sequencing (Lecture 11): questions

## 29 questions

### PCR extras: fingerprinting and qPCR

> [!question]- Who first developed DNA fingerprinting, and where?
> Prof Alec Jeffreys and colleagues at the University of Leicester, in the 1980s.

> [!question]- How is PCR used in modern DNA fingerprinting?
> Primers flanking short tandem repeat (STR) loci amplify them; alleles with different numbers of repeats give products of different length, separated by capillary electrophoresis.

> [!question]- What enzyme is needed to use RNA as a PCR template?
> Reverse transcriptase, which copies the RNA into cDNA.

> [!question]- How does a TaqMan probe report the amount of PCR product?
> The probe carries a reporter and a quencher; when the polymerase extends through it, it cleaves off the reporter, which is no longer quenched and fluoresces, so the light rises with the product.

### Why DNA sequencing matters

> [!question]- Name the three DNA manipulation topics covered in this block of BIO-4018A lectures.
> Recombinant DNA, the polymerase chain reaction and DNA sequencing.

### How Sanger sequencing works

> [!question]- Why does adding a ddNTP stop DNA synthesis?
> A ddNTP has no 3′-OH group, so DNA polymerase cannot join the next nucleotide to it.

> [!question]- What three things does DNA synthesis need?
> A template strand, a primer and dNTPs (plus DNA polymerase).

> [!question]- In Sanger sequencing, why are only small amounts of ddNTPs added?
> So that each strand stops at a random point, giving fragments that end at every position rather than all stopping at the first base.

> [!question]- When was dideoxy sequencing developed, and when did automated systems appear?
> 1977; automated systems about 1990.

> [!question]- How fast were the first automated sequencers?
> 24 lanes × 200 bases, about 5000 bases per day.

### The Human Genome Project

> [!question]- When were the first draft human genome sequences published, and by whom?
> 2001: the public Human Genome Project (Francis Collins) and Celera Genomics (J. Craig Venter).

> [!question]- How did the strategies of the public Human Genome Project and Celera differ?
> The public project used hierarchical (BAC-by-BAC) shotgun sequencing of overlapping BACs from a map; Celera used whole-genome shotgun sequencing of random fragments of the whole genome.

> [!question]- When was the finished human genome sequence completed?
> 2004 (the draft was 2001).

### Advances in sequencing technology

> [!question]- Put these in order of when they were used: capillary sequencers, manual gels, next-generation sequencers, automated slab gels.
> Manual gels, automated slab gels, capillary sequencers, next-generation sequencers.

> [!question]- How has the cost of sequencing changed as the rate increased?
> It has fallen dramatically: from about $10 per kb to under $0.01 per kb while rates rose over a billion-fold.

### How next-generation sequencing works

> [!question]- Outline the main steps of next-generation sequencing.
> Fragment the genomic DNA and select a size; ligate adapters to each end; immobilise on a surface and amplify each fragment by PCR (~1000 copies); sequence each by synthesis, detecting the base added at each step.

> [!question]- What is the key difference between NGS and Sanger sequencing?
> NGS sequences millions of fragments at once (massively parallel) on a surface by synthesis, with no in vivo cloning, instead of one reaction per tube separated by size.

> [!question]- Why are adapters ligated to the fragments in NGS?
> They give every fragment the same known ends, so the fragments can be attached to the surface and primed for amplification and sequencing.

### Pyrosequencing and reversible terminators

> [!question]- In pyrosequencing, what produces the flash of light?
> Incorporation of a nucleotide releases pyrophosphate, which sulfurylase converts to ATP; luciferase uses the ATP to oxidise luciferin, giving light.

> [!question]- In pyrosequencing, what happens if a pulsed dNTP is not complementary to the next template base?
> It is not incorporated, so no flash is seen, and apyrase degrades it.

> [!question]- How does pyrosequencing show two identical bases in a row?
> Both are incorporated in one pulse, giving a flash about twice as bright.

> [!question]- How long are pyrosequencing reads?
> 400 to 500 nucleotides.

> [!question]- Why do the nucleotides in reversible terminator sequencing carry a blocking group?
> So only one nucleotide is added per cycle; after its fluorescent colour is recorded the label and block are removed so the next one can be added.

### Third-generation and the future of sequencing

> [!question]- What are the three generations of DNA sequencing, with an example of each?
> First: Sanger (or Maxam–Gilbert); second (NGS): 454 pyrosequencing or Illumina; third (single-molecule, real-time): PacBio or Oxford Nanopore.

> [!question]- How does nanopore sequencing read DNA?
> Single-stranded DNA is passed through a protein nanopore and changes in the flow of ions through the pore reveal the base sequence.

> [!question]- Give one advantage and one disadvantage of PacBio single-molecule sequencing compared with Illumina.
> Advantage: very long reads (over 10 kb), no amplification bias, can detect methylation. Disadvantage: high error rate (~10%) and lower throughput.

> [!question]- Give a clinical application of DNA sequencing described by Green, Rubin and Olson.
> Non-invasive prenatal testing for trisomy 21 using cell-free fetal DNA in maternal blood (also diagnosing congenital conditions and liquid biopsies for cancer).

### Summary (DNA Sequencing)

> [!question]- Name four developments that made sequencing large genomes possible.
> Pure DNA polymerases, more sensitive nucleic acid detection, automated robotics and improved computer power.

> [!question]- Name three uses of DNA sequencing.
> De novo genome sequencing, targeted re-sequencing and transcriptome profiling.
