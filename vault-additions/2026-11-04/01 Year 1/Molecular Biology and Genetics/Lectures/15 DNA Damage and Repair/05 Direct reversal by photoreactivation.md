# Direct reversal by photoreactivation

> [!path] [[Molecular Biology and Genetics]] › [[DNA Damage and Repair (Lecture 15)]]

*Source: Lecture 15: DNA damage and repair (PowerPoint), slides 14, 16*

**[[Photoreactivation]]** is **direct reversal** of a pyrimidine dimer by the enzyme **[[Photolyase|photolyase]]**:

1. Photolyase binds DNA containing a pyrimidine dimer and **flips the dimer out** into its active-site pocket
2. The cofactor **methenyltetrahydrofolate (5,10-MTHF)** absorbs a photon of **blue light**
3. The excitation energy passes from 5,10-MTHF to the catalytic cofactor **FADH⁻**
4. Excited FADH⁻ **transfers an electron to the dimer**, splitting it back into two separate pyrimidines
5. The electron returns to the flavin radical, **regenerating FADH⁻**

- Damage sensing and repair need **a single protein**
- Photolyases are found in many prokaryotes and eukaryotes but **not in placental mammals**, so humans rely on NER for UV damage

> [!quote] Klug 12e, p. 355
> Although the enzyme will associate with a thymine dimer in the dark, it must absorb a photon of blue light to cleave the dimer.

## Questions
> [!question]- What single protein can repair thymine dimers?
> Photolyase (photoreactivation).

> [!question]- What two cofactors does photolyase use, and what does each do?
> 5,10-MTHF absorbs a photon of blue light and passes the energy on; FADH⁻ transfers an electron to the dimer to split it.

> [!question]- Why can humans not repair thymine dimers by photoreactivation?
> Placental mammals do not have photolyase, so they use nucleotide excision repair instead.
