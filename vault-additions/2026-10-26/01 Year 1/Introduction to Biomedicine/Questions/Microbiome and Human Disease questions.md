# Microbiome and Human Disease: questions

> [!path] [[Introduction to Biomedicine]] › [[Microbiome and Human Disease]]

## 28 questions

### Microbiota and microbiome

> [!question]- What is the difference between the microbiota and the microbiome?
> The microbiota is the microorganisms themselves; the microbiome is the microbes plus all their genes and genomes.

### Where our microbes live

> [!question]- Name four body sites that carry a normal flora.
> Any four of: skin, mucous membranes, upper respiratory tract, GI tract, urethral opening, external genitalia, vagina, ear canal, external eye.

> [!question]- Roughly how many genes are in the human microbiome compared with the human genome?
> Over 1,000,000 against about 23,000.

> [!question]- Give an archaeon and a eukaryote from the human microbiome.
> Methanobrevibacter smithii (gut archaeon) and Candida albicans (a fungus).

### Metagenomics and how composition varies

> [!question]- What is the difference between 16S and shotgun sequencing?
> 16S amplifies one marker gene to identify which microbes are present; shotgun sequences all the DNA, showing which microbes and which genes.

> [!question]- Where in the gut is microbial biomass highest, and roughly how high?
> The colon, about 10¹¹ cells per ml.

> [!question]- Why does the stomach carry so few microbes?
> Its very low pH (about 1.5 to 5) kills most of them.

### What the normal flora does for us

> [!question]- List three defects of germ-free mice.
> Any three of: poor nutrient absorption, underdeveloped intestines, vitamin deficiency, underdeveloped immune system, heightened sensitivity to pathogens.

> [!question]- Which two vitamins does the gut flora make?
> Vitamin K and vitamin B12.

> [!question]- How does the normal flora prevent colonisation by pathogens?
> By competing for attachment sites and essential nutrients, and by producing bacteriocins and peroxides.

> [!question]- What are cross-reactive antibodies, and how do they help?
> Antibodies raised against normal flora that also recognise related pathogens, preventing infection.

### Gut microbes at work

> [!question]- What does B. thetaiotaomicron produce from dietary fibre?
> Short-chain fatty acids (such as acetate) for the host, plus H₂, CO₂ and formate.

> [!question]- What does M. smithii do with fermentation by-products?
> It turns H₂ and CO₂ into methane.

> [!question]- Which bacterium causes stomach ulcers?
> Helicobacter pylori.

> [!question]- Give three routes by which gut microbes can affect the brain.
> Any three of: vagus nerve signalling, neuroactive compounds (GABA, serotonin pathways), immune/inflammatory signals, metabolites such as butyrate.

### The microbiome and medicine

> [!question]- How do antibiotics lead to C. difficile infection?
> They kill the competing normal flora (dysbiosis), letting C. difficile colonise the gut.

> [!question]- How do C. difficile toxins damage the gut?
> They glycosylate and inactivate Rho and Rac GTPases, disrupting the cytoskeleton and tight junctions.

> [!question]- How can intratumoural bacteria make cancer cells resistant to gemcitabine?
> Their cytidine deaminase converts gemcitabine into an inactive metabolite.

### How we acquire our flora

> [!question]- Name two early-life factors that shape the gut flora.
> Mode of delivery (vaginal vs C-section) and feeding (breast vs bottle); also gestational age and maternal factors.

> [!question]- When does the infant gut community shift most?
> At the switch to solid foods.

### The gut microbiome and obesity

> [!question]- How does the gut flora of ob/ob mice differ from lean mice?
> More Firmicutes and fewer Bacteroidetes.

> [!question]- What did the faecal transplant experiment show?
> Germ-free mice given microbiota from obese donors gained more fat than those given lean-donor microbiota, so the microbiome contributes to obesity.

> [!question]- What is the ob/ob mutation?
> Homozygous mutation in the leptin gene.

### Faecal microbiota transplants

> [!question]- What is a faecal microbiota transplant, and what is it used for?
> Transfer of a healthy donor's faecal flora (assisted colonisation), used for recurrent C. difficile diarrhoea.

> [!question]- What happened to the patient after the faecal transplant?
> Diarrhoea subsided within 2 days, and their flora resembled the donor's within 14 days.

### The microbiome in health and disease

> [!question]- What is dysbiosis?
> A disrupted, altered microbiome, linked to a range of diseases.

> [!question]- Name three diseases linked to an altered microbiome, and the organ affected.
> For example: IBD (gut), asthma (lung), type 2 diabetes (pancreas), eczema (skin), NAFLD (liver), autism (brain).

> [!question]- Why study microbiome function rather than just species?
> Different species can carry out the same processes; what matters for the host is which genes and pathways are active (metagenomics, metatranscriptomics, metabolomics).
