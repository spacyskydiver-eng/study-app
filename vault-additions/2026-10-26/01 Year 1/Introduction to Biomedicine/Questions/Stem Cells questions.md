# Stem Cells: questions

> [!path] [[Introduction to Biomedicine]] › [[Stem Cells]]

## 31 questions

### What stem cells are

> [!question]- What are the two defining properties of a stem cell?
> Self-renewal (making more stem cells) and differentiation (specialising into other cell types).

> [!question]- Put totipotent, pluripotent and multipotent in order of how many cell types they can form.
> Totipotent (everything, including placenta) > pluripotent (all body cells) > multipotent (cells of one tissue).

> [!question]- Which cell is totipotent?
> The zygote (fertilised egg).

### From fertilisation to organogenesis

> [!question]- What is the inner cell mass, and why does it matter for stem cell research?
> The cluster of cells inside the blastocyst (days 3 to 8). It is pluripotent and is where embryonic stem cells come from.

> [!question]- Name the three germ layers and one thing each forms.
> Ectoderm (skin, nervous system), mesoderm (muscle, skeleton, circulatory system), endoderm (gut lining, internal organs).

> [!question]- When does gastrulation happen, and what forms?
> Week 3; the three germ layers form and the primitive streak appears.

### Differentiation and tissue renewal

> [!question]- Why do adult tissues need stem cells?
> To replace cells lost through wear and tear, injury or disease.

> [!question]- Where do the stem cells that renew the gut lining sit?
> At the base of the intestinal crypts (Lgr5⁺ stem cells).

### Embryonic versus tissue stem cells

> [!question]- Give one advantage and one disadvantage of embryonic stem cells for therapy.
> Advantage: pluripotent and self-renew well in the lab. Disadvantage: genetically different from the patient (rejection), and destroying embryos is ethically contested.

> [!question]- Give one advantage and one disadvantage of tissue stem cells for therapy.
> Advantage: can come from the patient, so no rejection and fewer ethical issues. Disadvantage: only a few cell types, rare and hard to grow.

> [!question]- Name two sources of tissue stem cells.
> Any two of: bone marrow, muscle, skin, fetus, umbilical cord, placenta, amniotic fluid.

### Induced pluripotent stem cells

> [!question]- Which four reprogramming factors make iPS cells?
> Oct4, Sox2, Klf4 and c-Myc.

> [!question]- Describe somatic cell nuclear transfer.
> Remove the nucleus from an egg, inject a nucleus from a somatic cell, let it divide to a blastocyst, then isolate ES cells.

> [!question]- Give two advantages of iPS cells over ES cells.
> No embryo is destroyed, and cells from the patient avoid immune rejection.

> [!question]- Name three uses of iPS cells.
> Disease modelling, drug screening and therapy.

### Stem cells and immune rejection

> [!question]- Why would ES cell therapies risk rejection?
> The cells are genetically different from the recipient, so the immune system sees them as foreign.

> [!question]- When do tissue stem cell or iPS cell therapies need immune-suppressing drugs?
> When the starting cells come from a donor rather than the patient.

### Stem cell therapy in practice, the tissue-engineered windpipe

> [!question]- In the windpipe case, what came from the donor and what from the patient?
> The donor gave the trachea, stripped to a connective tissue scaffold; the patient gave the stem cells that regrew the cellular part.

> [!question]- Why did the windpipe patient not need immune-suppressing drugs?
> The cells on the scaffold were the patient's own, so they were not rejected.

### Future prospects, iPS cell therapy

> [!question]- Outline the steps of iPS cell therapy for a blood disorder.
> Collect skin cells, reprogram to iPS cells, correct the mutation, differentiate into blood stem cells, transplant back.

> [!question]- Which disease is the example in the lecture?
> Fanconi anaemia (shown with a sickle cell anaemia mouse).

### From tissue stem cells to cancer

> [!question]- Why can a tumour relapse after conventional therapy?
> Surviving cancer stem cells self-renew and regrow the tumour.

> [!question]- What happens to a tumour if its cancer stem cells are destroyed?
> The remaining cells cannot self-renew, so the tumour degenerates.

### Organoids, personalised medicine and gut stem cell transplants

> [!question]- What are cryptoids and tumouroids?
> Organoids grown from a patient's normal gut tissue and tumour tissue respectively.

> [!question]- How do organoids support personalised medicine?
> Drugs are tested on the patient's own tumour organoids, and sequencing links mutations to drug response.

### Stem cells and UK law

> [!question]- Which act governs embryo research in the UK, and which body licenses it?
> The Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 1990 (amended 2000, 2008); licences come from the HFEA.

> [!question]- What is the 14-day rule?
> No human embryo can be kept or used for research beyond 14 days.

> [!question]- Is there an international law on stem cell research?
> No. Each country has its own laws.

### The ethics of embryonic stem cell research

> [!question]- Give the three views on the moral status of an embryo.
> No moral status; the same status as a baby; an intermediate view.

> [!question]- Name two ways embryos are created for research in the UK.
> Animal-human hybrid embryos and nuclear replacement.

### Summary and further reading

> [!question]- Name three ways stem cells are used in research and medicine.
> Cell replacement therapy, drug discovery, drug toxicity testing.
