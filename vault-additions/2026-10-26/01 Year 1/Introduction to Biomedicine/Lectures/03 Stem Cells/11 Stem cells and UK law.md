# Stem cells and UK law

> [!path] [[Introduction to Biomedicine]] › [[Stem Cells]]

*Source: Introduction to Biomedicine, Grand Challenges: Stem cells, Dr Mark Williams (PowerPoint), slides 27-31*

- Law sets what society accepts, protects us from harm, and promotes social goods such as welfare and economic growth
- The central question: should research that uses (and destroys) human embryos be allowed, even if it might lead to cures for diseases like cancer and Alzheimer's?
- **UK: the [[Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act|Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act]] (1990, amended 2000 and 2008)**
  - A **balanced approach**: protects the embryo as a human organism while allowing research under strict controls
  - Embryos may only be used (and destroyed) for specific purposes, such as fertility treatment or research into human illnesses
  - **No embryo can be kept or used after 14 days** ([[14-day rule]])
  - Anyone creating, storing or using embryos outside the body is covered, and embryonic stem cell research needs a **licence from the HFEA** (Human Fertilisation and Embryology Authority)
- **No international law** on stem cell research; national laws differ, reflecting different views of the embryo
- **Cost and patents**: companies rely on patents for profit, but in **Europe** patents are unlikely to be granted for ES cell inventions that destroy embryos

## Questions
> [!question]- Which act governs embryo research in the UK, and which body licenses it?
> The Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 1990 (amended 2000, 2008); licences come from the HFEA.

> [!question]- What is the 14-day rule?
> No human embryo can be kept or used for research beyond 14 days.

> [!question]- Is there an international law on stem cell research?
> No. Each country has its own laws.
