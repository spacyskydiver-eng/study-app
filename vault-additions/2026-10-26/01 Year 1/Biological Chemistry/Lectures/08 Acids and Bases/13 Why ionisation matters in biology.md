# Why ionisation matters in biology

> [!path] [[Biological Chemistry]] › [[Acids and Bases]]

*Source: Biological Chemistry, Chemistry in Aqueous Environment: Acids and Bases, Dr Maria Vardakou (PowerPoint); Crowe and Bradshaw, Chemistry for the Biosciences, ch. 17, slides 47-51*

- **Binding**: some bonds need charges (ionic bonds, salt bridges, e.g. in the Kelch domain of Keap1); others only form between neutral groups (such as the hydrogen-bonded pairs between carboxylic acids)
- **Chemical reactions**: many only happen if an acid or base is in the right protonation state, e.g. the two aspartates in the active site of **pepsin**, or **labelling lysine** with an NHS-ester fluorophore (the –NH₂ must be deprotonated to act as a nucleophile)
- **Solubility**: the charged form dissolves in **water**, the neutral form in **organic solvent** (the basis of acid-base extraction in a separating funnel)
- **Membrane permeability**: only the **neutral** form crosses the lipid bilayer. Paracetamol crosses when neutral but not when protonated and charged
- **Drug absorption, aspirin**: aspirin is a carboxylic acid. In the **acidic stomach** (low pH) it is protonated and neutral, so it **crosses into cells**; at higher pH it is deprotonated and charged, and stays out

## Questions
> [!question]- Why must a drug be uncharged to cross a cell membrane?
> The lipid bilayer's hydrophobic core repels charged species; only the neutral form diffuses through.

> [!question]- Why is aspirin absorbed well in the stomach?
> At the stomach's low pH aspirin (an acid) is protonated and neutral, so it crosses membranes.

> [!question]- Give two other reasons ionisation state matters.
> Any two of: binding (ionic bonds need charges), reactivity (e.g. lysine labelling, enzyme active sites), solubility in water vs solvent.
