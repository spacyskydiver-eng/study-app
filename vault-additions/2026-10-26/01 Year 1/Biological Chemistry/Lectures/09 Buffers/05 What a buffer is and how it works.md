# What a buffer is and how it works

> [!path] [[Biological Chemistry]] › [[Buffers]]

*Source: Biological Chemistry, Chemistry in Aqueous Environment: Importance of Buffers, Dr Maria Vardakou (PowerPoint); Crowe and Bradshaw, Chemistry for the Biosciences, slides 11-16*

- A **[[Buffer|buffer]]** is a mixture of a **weak acid and its conjugate base**, or a **weak base and its conjugate acid**, which **resists changes in pH**
- In plain water, adding acid makes the pH drop sharply
- **Example: acetic acid + sodium acetate**
  - CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + CH₃COO⁻ and CH₃COONa → CH₃COO⁻ + Na⁺
  - **Add acid**: H₃O⁺ is consumed by **acetate** (CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺ → CH₃COOH + H₂O), so **the pH does not drop**
  - **Add base**: OH⁻ is consumed by **acetic acid** (CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O), so **the pH does not rise**
- The added ions are used up and so are not free to change the pH
- **[[Buffering capacity]]** matters: the buffer only works while **[buffer] ≫ added [H₃O⁺] or [OH⁻]**

## Questions
> [!question]- What is a buffer?
> A mixture of a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak base and its conjugate acid) that resists changes in pH.

> [!question]- In an acetate buffer, which component neutralises added acid?
> Acetate (CH₃COO⁻), which takes up the H₃O⁺ to form acetic acid.

> [!question]- What happens when you add more acid than the buffer can absorb?
> The buffering capacity is exceeded, and the pH falls sharply.
