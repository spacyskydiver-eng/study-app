# Acids and Bases: questions

> [!path] [[Biological Chemistry]] › [[Acids and Bases]]

## 37 questions

### Defining acids, Arrhenius

> [!question]- State the Arrhenius definitions of an acid and a base.
> An acid dissolves in water to produce H⁺; a base dissolves in water to produce OH⁻.

> [!question]- What is the main limitation of the Arrhenius definition?
> It only applies to aqueous solutions.

### Brønsted-Lowry acids and the hydronium ion

> [!question]- Define a Brønsted-Lowry acid and base.
> An acid is a proton (H⁺) donor; a base is a proton acceptor.

> [!question]- Why do we write H₃O⁺ rather than H⁺ in water?
> A free H⁺ is too reactive; it immediately bonds to a water molecule to form the hydronium ion.

### Monoprotic, diprotic and triprotic acids

> [!question]- Classify HCl, H₂SO₄ and H₃PO₄ by proton yield.
> HCl monoprotic, H₂SO₄ diprotic, H₃PO₄ triprotic.

> [!question]- Write the two dissociation steps of sulfuric acid.
> H₂SO₄ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + HSO₄⁻; HSO₄⁻ + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + SO₄²⁻.

### Amphoteric species

> [!question]- What does amphoteric mean?
> Able to act as an acid or a base.

> [!question]- In NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻, is water acting as an acid or a base?
> An acid: it donates a proton to NH₃.

> [!question]- Which groups make an amino acid amphoteric?
> The carboxyl group (proton donor) and the amino group (proton acceptor).

### Conjugate acid-base pairs

> [!question]- What is the conjugate base of CH₃COOH?
> CH₃COO⁻ (acetate).

> [!question]- Identify both conjugate pairs in NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻.
> NH₄⁺/NH₃ and H₂O/OH⁻.

> [!question]- What is the conjugate acid of water?
> H₃O⁺.

### pH and pOH

> [!question]- A solution has [H₃O⁺] = 10⁻⁴ M. What is its pH and pOH?
> pH 4, pOH 10.

> [!question]- How much more acidic is pH 3 than pH 5?
> 100 times (two pH units, 10² in [H₃O⁺]).

> [!question]- Can pH be negative?
> Yes, for strong acids above 1 M, e.g. 10 M HCl has pH −1.

### Strong and weak acids, Ka and Kb

> [!question]- Write the Ka expression for HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻.
> Ka = [H₃O⁺][A⁻] / [HA].

> [!question]- Why is [H₂O] left out of Ka?
> Water is in vast excess (55.5 M) and its concentration barely changes.

> [!question]- What does a small Ka tell you?
> A weak acid: the equilibrium lies to the left and little H⁺ is released.

### pKa and pKb

> [!question]- Acid X has pKa 3, acid Y pKa 9. Which is stronger?
> X: lower pKa means stronger acid.

> [!question]- Convert Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ to pKa.
> pKa = −log(1.8 × 10⁻⁵) = 4.74 (≈ 4.75).

> [!question]- What sort of acid has a negative pKa?
> A very strong acid (Ka > 1), such as HCl.

### The pH of a strong acid

> [!question]- What is the pH of 0.01 M HCl?
> pH 2 (complete dissociation, [H₃O⁺] = 10⁻² M).

> [!question]- Why can you use pH = −log[acid] for HCl but not for acetic acid?
> HCl dissociates completely; acetic acid only partly, so [H₃O⁺] is much less than [acid].

### The pH of a weak acid

> [!question]- Set up the Ka expression for a weak acid of concentration c using an ICE table.
> Ka = x² / (c − x), where x = [H₃O⁺] = [A⁻].

> [!question]- Why do you discard one root of the quadratic?
> It is negative, and a concentration cannot be negative.

> [!question]- Find the pH of 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ M acetic acid (Ka 1.8 × 10⁻⁵).
> x = 4.4 × 10⁻⁵ M, pH = 4.36.

### Kw and the pH of bases

> [!question]- What is Kw at 25 °C?
> 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ M² ([H₃O⁺][OH⁻]); pKw = 14.

> [!question]- Find the pH of 0.001 M NaOH.
> [OH⁻] = 10⁻³, pOH 3, pH 11.

> [!question]- How do you find the pH of a weak base?
> Use Kb and an ICE table to find x = [OH⁻], then pOH = −log x and pH = 14 − pOH.

### Protonation state and pH

> [!question]- Ibuprofen (acid, pKa 5.2) at pH 2: protonated? charged?
> pH < pKa, so protonated and neutral (–COOH).

> [!question]- Ketamine (base, pKa 7.5) at pH 2: protonated? charged?
> pH < pKa, so protonated and charged (–NH₂⁺–).

> [!question]- Benzocaine (base, pKa 2.8) at pH 5: protonated? charged?
> pH > pKa, so deprotonated and neutral (–NH₂).

> [!question]- Isoleucine (pKa 2.26 and 9.6) at pH 7: what form is it in?
> The carboxyl is deprotonated (–COO⁻) and the amino group protonated (–NH₃⁺): a zwitterion, charged groups but no net charge.

> [!question]- An acid is in a solution with pH above its pKa. Is it charged?
> Yes: it is deprotonated (A⁻), so negatively charged.

### Why ionisation matters in biology

> [!question]- Why must a drug be uncharged to cross a cell membrane?
> The lipid bilayer's hydrophobic core repels charged species; only the neutral form diffuses through.

> [!question]- Why is aspirin absorbed well in the stomach?
> At the stomach's low pH aspirin (an acid) is protonated and neutral, so it crosses membranes.

> [!question]- Give two other reasons ionisation state matters.
> Any two of: binding (ionic bonds need charges), reactivity (e.g. lysine labelling, enzyme active sites), solubility in water vs solvent.
