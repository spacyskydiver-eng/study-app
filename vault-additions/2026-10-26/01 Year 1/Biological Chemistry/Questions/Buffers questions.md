# Buffers: questions

> [!path] [[Biological Chemistry]] › [[Buffers]]

## 23 questions

### Recap, protonation and pH

> [!question]- A weak base is in solution at a pH below its pKa. Is it charged?
> Yes: it is protonated (BH⁺), so positively charged.

### The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation

> [!question]- Write the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation.
> pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]).

> [!question]- Which equilibrium constant is the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation derived from?
> The acid dissociation constant, Ka = [H₃O⁺][A⁻]/[HA].

### Using the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation

> [!question]- An acid has pKa 6. At pH 6, what fraction is deprotonated?
> 50%: when pH = pKa, [A⁻] = [HA].

> [!question]- Acetic acid (pKa 4.75) at pH 3.75: what is [A⁻]/[HA]?
> 10^(3.75 − 4.75) = 10⁻¹ = 0.1, so about 9% deprotonated.

> [!question]- Rearrange Henderson-Hasselbalch to give the ratio [A⁻]/[HA].
> [A⁻]/[HA] = 10^(pH − pKa).

### pKa and titration curves

> [!question]- What is the pH at the midpoint of a weak acid titration?
> pH = pKa, because [HA] = [A⁻].

> [!question]- Over what range does acetic acid (pKa 4.76) buffer?
> Roughly pH 3.76 to 5.76 (pKa ± 1).

### What a buffer is and how it works

> [!question]- What is a buffer?
> A mixture of a weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak base and its conjugate acid) that resists changes in pH.

> [!question]- In an acetate buffer, which component neutralises added acid?
> Acetate (CH₃COO⁻), which takes up the H₃O⁺ to form acetic acid.

> [!question]- What happens when you add more acid than the buffer can absorb?
> The buffering capacity is exceeded, and the pH falls sharply.

### Rules for making a buffer

> [!question]- Why can't HCl and NaCl make a buffer?
> HCl is a strong acid; Cl⁻ is too weak a base to take H⁺ back, so there is no equilibrium to absorb added acid or base.

> [!question]- Which salt would you pair with ammonia to make a buffer?
> Ammonium chloride (the salt of its conjugate acid, NH₄⁺).

### Why we need buffers

> [!question]- Write the bicarbonate buffer equilibrium in blood.
> CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ H₂CO₃ ⇌ H₃O⁺ + HCO₃⁻.

> [!question]- Give two reasons buffers are used in lab experiments.
> Any two of: keep proteins and cells stable; hold the optimum pH; stop reagents or products changing the pH.

### Choosing a buffer

> [!question]- You need a buffer at pH 7.5. Would acetate (pKa 4.76) work? Suggest a better one.
> No: 7.5 is far outside 4.76 ± 1. HEPES (pKa 7.48) or Tris (8.06) would work.

> [!question]- What is the rule of thumb for a buffer's working range?
> Within 1 pH unit of its pKa.

### Calculating the pH of a buffer

> [!question]- What is the total concentration of a buffer made from 0.05 M acetic acid and 0.15 M sodium acetate?
> 0.20 M.

> [!question]- In an ammonia / ammonium chloride buffer, which is the "acid" in Henderson-Hasselbalch?
> Ammonium chloride (NH₄⁺).

> [!question]- Find the pH of a buffer of 0.1 M acetic acid and 0.1 M sodium acetate (pKa 4.75).
> pH = 4.75 + log(1) = 4.75.

### Calculating the concentrations for a buffer

> [!question]- Make 0.1 M acetate buffer at pH 4.75 (pKa 4.75). What are the concentrations?
> Ratio = 10⁰ = 1, so 0.05 M acetic acid and 0.05 M sodium acetate.

> [!question]- Make 1 L of 0.1 M acetate buffer at pH 4.5 (pKa 4.75).
> Ratio 10^(−0.25) = 0.562; acetic acid 0.0640 M, sodium acetate 0.0360 M.

> [!question]- Which two equations do you solve to find buffer component concentrations?
> [base]/[acid] = 10^(pH − pKa) and [base] + [acid] = total concentration.
